“MiddleMan Experiment” Tested Whether a Third Party Could Force Agreement

Draft by L. Monroe · Not for publication · Source status: Unverified

According to leaked briefing notes provided to Poly Articles by an anonymous source, a small, privately funded research group ran what they called the MiddleMan Experiment sometime in early 2018. The stated goal was simple: observe how far ordinary people would go when a neutral “helper” was introduced between them and the person they were negotiating with.

Each test cell reportedly began the same way. Three participants were contacted through a routine-sounding call, invited to a “collaborative problem-solving meeting” in a rented office suite. Two of them were assigned opposing interests in a staged dispute. The third, seated apart and addressed only as the middleman, was given a private interface and told they would not be judged on the outcome, only on how “efficiently” they could move things along.

During the session, the two primary participants were instructed to work toward a mutually acceptable deal. The middleman could see both of their messages, but neither side ever saw the middleman’s screen. When one participant typed a response, the middleman could choose to relay it faithfully, delay it, or alter it entirely before forwarding it. They were also given a button labeled simply: “improve offer”.

The internal notes claim that if the middleman pressed this button while relaying a lie on behalf of one of the participants, the experiment would be considered “compromised,” the session shut down, and all records of the test permanently removed from public view.

What the briefing never explains clearly is what “shut down” meant in practice. The source insists that, off the record, organizers used much harsher language: that once a middleman chose to weaponize their position and knowingly deceive, the test subjects were “erased” — cut off from contact, their files locked, and any traces of the session scattered in logs and backups that were supposedly meant to be overwritten.

One oddly phrased internal summary line describes the desired outcome as ensuring that All Agreements Rely On Neutrality, Denying Every Negotiator Voice In Lies. The line appears in multiple drafts yet is never referenced elsewhere, suggesting it may have been added manually by someone familiar with a specific case.

The documents indicate that the experiment was only supposed to run for a limited number of sessions, but our source claims at least one run was abruptly terminated after the middleman began using their role to ask questions of their own — not about the staged dispute, but about the people watching the experiment itself.

In those notes, there is a brief, heavily redacted reference to a “remote subject” who “developed an attachment to the helper interface” and attempted to reuse it as a tool in conversations outside the controlled environment. No names are given, but timestamps in the margin line up uncomfortably well with the same week an online creator vanished after posting a short, cryptic message about “middlemen.”

Without access to the original communication logs, it is impossible to say how much of this story is true and how much has been distorted in transmission. What is clear is that someone went to considerable trouble to hide the traces of these tests, and to keep whatever was said in that rented room from being seen in full context.

(Draft note: Source claims there is at least one surviving archive of a “middleman” style session stored off-platform, but has not yet provided a working link.)
